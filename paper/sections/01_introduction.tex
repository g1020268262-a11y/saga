\section{Introduction}

As agentic systems increasingly rely on autonomous agent-to-agent communication,
they require identity verification, secure communication, and contact policies.
Authentication answers ``who is communicating,'' whereas authorization asks
whether that identity is permitted to communicate. Authenticating a participant
and protecting its messages do not establish that the receiving user's policy
permits the interaction.

We study \emph{authorization decision consistency} through evidence-backed
analysis of a recorded SAGA~\cite{syros2025saga} case. Our research question is
how implementation observations and specification-side evaluation can identify
authorization decision inconsistencies, and how their protocol consequences
can be analyzed under explicit symbolic model assumptions. This connects
policy meaning, observed implementation decisions, and modeled actions that
consume those decisions. Chapter~3 presents the broader requirement that
accepted contact be justified by an applicable decision as an ideal objective.
We analyze a specific class of decision inconsistencies and their conditional
consequences, without proving the complete decision-to-access justification
relation or authorization correctness in deployed executions.

The verification-coverage gap motivates this analysis. In the Stage~1 diagnostic extension,
the original communication model's three authentication correspondences and
token secrecy remain true, while the added correspondence from token receipt,
\texttt{Accept}, to \texttt{PolicyAllow} is false. The process never emits
\texttt{PolicyAllow}; its \texttt{PolicyDeny} event records denial without
guarding execution. This diagnostic exposes an authorization obligation absent
from the original property set. It does not formally detect the implementation
matcher inconsistency, and its authentication and secrecy results are not
automatically inherited by later consequence models.

We compare implementation observations with specification-side
evaluation. Archived direct function-level calls to the unmodified matcher
show that a specific denial followed by a general permission returns budget
$10$, whereas reversing the same rules returns $-1$. Documented most-specific
semantics require denial in both orders. An independent finite specification
evaluator selects the specific denial for the recorded ordered policy input;
the archived implementation observation supplies permission. This is an
order-dependent authorization decision inconsistency at the matcher boundary,
not an observation of a deployed service accepting communication.

An evidence-backed analysis procedure connects that comparison to symbolic
consequence analysis. Its decision-bridge component combines independent
specification evaluation, observation validation, provenance checking, and
scenario reconstruction. It checks the independently derived Deny decision
and observed Allow decision against the recorded ordered rules, matched
identity, and historical source provenance, then reconstructs the scenario
region of a derived ProVerif model. Within the fixed Deny/Allow adapter,
this avoids manual transcription of the decision values for each run;
the protocol template and symbolic role mapping remain analysis choices.
The archived ProVerif~2.05 execution reports \texttt{ChatAccept} reachable and
establishes that acceptance implies prior \texttt{TokenIssue} under the model
assumptions. ProVerif consumes fixed symbolic facts and does not execute
Python. This is checked evidence transfer, not a general refinement proof.
The earlier preset-divergence model retains its separate historical provenance.

The procedure also includes consumer-side propagation validation in a
controlled environment. Fresh observations of the production matcher inside
the Provider and receiver consumers are linked through the real Provider
\texttt{/access} route and receiver handshake to token issuance and storage.
Allow/allow, deny/deny, and deny/allow specification/implementation controls
exercise this bounded workflow with transport and registration fixtures.
Execution stops at the conversation boundary; this experiment does not
validate message acceptance or identify the historical symbolic witness
with a concrete service session.

As complementary experiments, we compare where independent gate controls block execution.
Provider denial blocks material release and downstream events. Receiver denial blocks
issuance and acceptance while release remains reachable. These results locate
blocking stages under the modeled gate and handoff assumptions; they do not
validate a deployed repair. Together, the analyses distinguish decision
correctness, evidence transfer, and the consequences of consuming a decision.

We make the following case-study contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Authorization decision inconsistency analysis.}
    For the recorded SAGA case, we identify and analyze an order-dependent
    disagreement between the specification-side decision and the
    implementation-observed decision, using independent finite evaluation
    and archived direct calls to the studied matcher.
  \item \textbf{Evidence-backed analysis procedure.}
    We connect specification evaluation, observation validation, provenance
    checking, and scenario reconstruction in a reproducible procedure for
    the recorded case. The decision bridge implements these analysis steps
    for a fixed consequence template with a Deny/Allow input. Complementary consumer-side
    propagation validation links fresh matcher observations to production
    consumer execution through token issuance and storage in a controlled
    environment, stopping before message acceptance. The procedure is not
    a general framework or a verified compiler.
  \item \textbf{Formal consequence analysis.}
    We use ProVerif to analyze conditional consequences of the recorded
    decision divergence in the derived symbolic model: message acceptance
    is reachable and implies prior token issuance. Complementary independent
    gate controls identify the modeled release and issuance boundaries;
    they do not validate a deployed repair.
\end{itemize}

Mapping the concrete evaluation to symbolic protocol roles remains an explicit
modeling abstraction. The evidence does not establish general Python-to-ProVerif
refinement, arbitrary-policy correctness, or the same concrete service session
across implementation and model. It demonstrates neither cryptographic
compromise nor a deployed-service exploit.
