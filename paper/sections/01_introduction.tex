\section{Introduction}

% Background: paper/PAPER_STORY.md, Section 1;
% paper/sections/03_problem_formulation.tex, Authentication Is Not Authorization.
As agentic systems increasingly rely on autonomous agent-to-agent communication,
they require identity verification, secure communication, and contact policies:
authentication answers ``who is communicating,'' whereas authorization answers
``who should be allowed to communicate.'' Authenticating a participant and
protecting its messages do not establish that a user's policy permits that
participant to communicate.

% Problem: proofs/CLAIM_EVIDENCE_MATRIX.md, Claim 1;
% paper/sections/03_problem_formulation.tex, Policy--Authorization Consistency.
Authentication and confidentiality verification can establish credential origin
and secrecy without establishing authorization correctness. That additional
guarantee requires consistency among policy semantics, implementation, and
protocol enforcement. We investigate this gap through a SAGA case study, asking
whether its authorization pipeline preserves
\emph{policy--authorization consistency}: accepted contact must be justified by
an applicable decision under the receiving user's intended policy semantics.

% Observation: proofs/CLAIM_EVIDENCE_MATRIX.md, Claims 1--2;
% proofs/AUDIT_STATUS.md, Stages 1 and 3.
In the diagnostic extension of SAGA's reference communication model, the
existing authentication and token-secrecy queries remain satisfied, while an
added authorization correspondence fails. This result identifies a proof
coverage gap at token receipt. Separately, the contact policy matcher can allow
contact where its documented most-specific-rule semantics require denial.
Together, these observations motivate examining the chain from policy
specification, through matcher implementation and authorization decision, to
protocol capability, with separate evidence for each boundary.

% Approach: proofs/CLAIM_EVIDENCE_MATRIX.md, Claims 2--4;
% proofs/AUDIT_STATUS.md, Stages 2--4.
Our analysis has three steps. First, archived direct calls to the matcher show
order-dependent decisions: a general permission can override a specific denial.
Second, we use ProVerif to analyze the consequences. The formal model
abstracts the mismatch using fixed scenario facts: the specification denies
contact while the implementation allows it. It does not execute the Python
matcher. A saved trace reaches
token issuance (\texttt{TokenIssue}) and message acceptance
(\texttt{ChatAccept}) under these assumptions. Third, we compare Provider and
receiver authorization gates:
Provider denial prevents material release, whereas receiver denial can prevent
issuance after release, under the modeled gate and handoff assumptions.
A strict semantic mapping from concrete matcher execution
to formal events remains unfinished; service-level end-to-end reproduction is
outside the completed evidence.

% Contributions: paper/PAPER_STORY.md, Section 6;
% proofs/CLAIM_EVIDENCE_MATRIX.md, Claims 1--4.
Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We identify an authorization enforcement gap between cryptographic
    verification and policy enforcement in agentic systems through the SAGA
    case study.
  \item We demonstrate an implementation-level, order-dependent authorization
    inconsistency in SAGA's contact policy matcher.
  \item We formally analyze how authorization divergence can affect agent
    communication authorization under explicit modeling assumptions, including
    the effect of Provider and receiver gate placement.
\end{itemize}
