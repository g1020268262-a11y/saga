\section{Introduction}

As agentic systems increasingly rely on autonomous agent-to-agent communication,
they require identity verification, secure communication, and contact policies.
Authentication answers ``who is communicating,'' whereas authorization asks
whether that identity is permitted to communicate. Authenticating a participant
and protecting its messages do not establish that the receiving user's policy
permits the interaction.

We study \emph{authorization decision consistency}: whether the authorization
state consumed by enforcement was derived according to the intended policy
semantics. This connects policy meaning, implementation evaluation, and the
protocol actions that consume a decision. Through a SAGA~\cite{syros2025saga}
case study, we investigate what evidence is needed to justify that connection.
Chapter~3 formulates the broader policy--authorization consistency requirement:
accepted contact must be justified by an applicable decision linked to the
relevant participants and credential. Our evidence addresses specified parts
of that requirement, rather than proving the complete deployed pipeline.

First, we diagnose verification coverage. In the Stage~1 diagnostic extension,
the original communication model's three authentication correspondences and
token secrecy remain true, while the added correspondence from token receipt,
\texttt{Accept}, to \texttt{PolicyAllow} is false. The process never emits
\texttt{PolicyAllow}; its \texttt{PolicyDeny} event records denial without
guarding execution. This diagnostic exposes an authorization obligation absent
from the original property set. It does not formally detect the implementation
matcher inconsistency, and its authentication and secrecy results are not
automatically inherited by later consequence models.

Second, we compare implementation observations with specification-side
evaluation. Archived direct function-level calls to the unmodified matcher
show that a specific denial followed by a general permission returns budget
$10$, whereas reversing the same rules returns $-1$. Documented most-specific
semantics require denial in both orders. An independent finite specification
evaluator selects the specific denial for the recorded ordered policy input;
the archived implementation observation supplies permission. This is an
order-dependent authorization decision inconsistency at the matcher boundary,
not an observation of a deployed service accepting communication.

Third, a provenance-bound decision bridge connects the recorded evaluation
to symbolic consequence analysis. It checks the independently derived Deny
decision and observed Allow decision against the same concrete evaluation
context, then reconstructs the scenario region of a derived ProVerif model.
A fresh ProVerif~2.05 execution reports \texttt{ChatAccept} reachable and
establishes that acceptance implies prior \texttt{TokenIssue}. The bridge
removes hand-entered decision labels for this case; ProVerif still consumes
fixed symbolic facts and does not execute Python. This is checked evidence
transfer, not a general refinement proof. The earlier preset-divergence model
retains its separate historical provenance.

Fourth, we compare where the independent gate controls block execution.
Provider denial blocks material release and downstream events. Receiver denial blocks
issuance and acceptance while release remains reachable. These results locate
blocking stages under the modeled gate and handoff assumptions; they do not
validate a deployed repair. Together, the analyses distinguish decision
correctness, evidence transfer, and the consequences of consuming a decision.

We make the following case-study contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We characterize an authorization proof-coverage gap in SAGA's
    communication model, separating the queried authentication and secrecy
    guarantees from the required authorization relation.
  \item We demonstrate an order-dependent authorization decision inconsistency
    through function-level observations of the studied contact-policy matcher,
    contrasted with its documented most-specific semantics.
  \item We provide a reproducible decision bridge for the recorded evaluation:
    independent finite specification evaluation and a provenance-bound archived
    observation form a checked pair used to reconstruct the formal scenario.
    This mechanism is not a verified compiler or complete policy semantics.
  \item We analyze symbolic consequences in the derived model: message
    acceptance is reachable and implies prior issuance. Separate gate controls
    identify the modeled release and issuance boundaries.
\end{itemize}

Mapping the concrete evaluation to symbolic protocol roles remains an explicit
modeling abstraction. The evidence does not establish general Python-to-ProVerif
refinement, arbitrary-policy correctness, or the same concrete service session
across implementation and model. It demonstrates neither cryptographic
compromise nor a deployed-service exploit.
