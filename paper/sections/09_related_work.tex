\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Agent Security and Governance}

Research on autonomous agents examines both how agents act and how their
interactions can be made trustworthy. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2024agents}
organize work on LLM-based agents around their construction, applications,
and evaluation. This architectural perspective provides context for agents
that plan and use external capabilities, but is not itself an implementation
verification of a contact-policy evaluator. Earlier work by Ramchurn et
al.~\cite{ramchurn2004trust} surveys trust in multi-agent systems as a means
of managing uncertainty in interactions. Trust assessment concerns
expectations about another agent's behavior; an authorization rule instead
specifies whether a particular interaction is permitted.

AgentDojo~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo} studies agent security through
an extensible environment for evaluating prompt injection attacks and
defenses when agents use tools over untrusted data. Its threat model and
behavioral evaluation address whether externally supplied content can redirect
an agent's actions. This is complementary to the governance question studied
here: whether a software decision that grants communication agrees with the
receiving user's policy. Our case does not require a prompt injection or an
LLM to misinterpret an instruction.

These works motivate scrutiny of agent behavior, coordination, and trust
without implying that they establish every authorization property of a
framework. We focus on consistency between policy evaluation and enforcement
for contact by an identified agent. The distinction is in the object of
analysis, not a finding that the cited approaches fail at their own goals.

\subsection{Capability and Token-Based Authorization}

Credential systems provide explicit representations of authority and ways to
delegate or restrict it. The SPKI certificate theory~\cite{ellison1999spki}
connects names and authorizations to keys, with rules for deriving authority
from certificates and access-control lists. Its treatment of SDSI names and
authorization certificates already distinguishes naming a principal from
granting that principal permission. Macaroons~\cite{birgisson2014macaroons}
support decentralized delegation using credentials that can be restricted
with contextual caveats. Such attenuation constrains the authority exercised
through a credential rather than simply asserting an identity.

OAuth~2.0~\cite{hardt2012oauth} specifies a framework for obtaining limited
access to HTTP services, separating the resource owner, client, authorization
server, and resource server. Access tokens represent authorization with scope
and duration; OAuth does not prescribe a particular token representation.
We cite RFC~6749 as the foundational framework, not as a complete account of
subsequent OAuth security guidance. SPKI is an Experimental RFC and OAuth is
a Standards Track RFC; they are published specifications rather than
conference papers.

These mechanisms address credential meaning, delegation, and restrictions;
they do not all use the same capability model. Their relevance to SAGA is
that credential validation and the correctness of the decision producing
authority are separate obligations. A valid credential does not, by its
existence alone, establish that an external policy evaluator implemented the
intended rule semantics. We examine that evaluator-to-protocol relationship,
not weaknesses in SPKI, OAuth, or Macaroons. The case study introduces neither
a credential format nor an attenuation mechanism.

\subsection{Formal Verification of Authentication Protocols}

Blanchet's account of ProVerif~\cite{blanchet2016proverif} describes symbolic
verification using the applied pi calculus, including secrecy,
authentication, and equivalence properties. Authentication can be expressed
through correspondences between protocol events. The Tamarin prover of
Meier, Schmidt, Cremers, and Basin~\cite{meier2013tamarin} represents
protocol behavior with multiset rewriting and checks trace properties under
specified adversary and equational models. Its rules and trace lemmas can
express stateful behavior as well as authentication and secrecy requirements.
Neither method is intrinsically limited to identity authentication.

The connection to our work is therefore one of verification scope. A result
about message origin or adversarial knowledge does not automatically include
a policy-evaluation obligation absent from the modeled behavior and queries.
Conversely, explicitly modeling authorization can make such obligations
amenable to protocol analysis. Our coverage diagnostic retains the original
queried authentication and secrecy results while identifying the missing
authorization basis; it does not challenge the soundness of the verifier.

We use ProVerif for a conditional consequence analysis with explicit
specification-deny and implementation-allow assumptions. The resulting
reachability witness concerns token issuance and message acceptance in that
model. It is not execution of the Python matcher or a proof of refinement
from the implementation. We do not compare ProVerif and Tamarin experimentally
or claim that changing tools would remove this modeling boundary.

\subsection{Formal Verification of Authorization Policies}

Authorization languages and policy-analysis tools directly address permission
semantics. Cassandra~\cite{becker2004cassandra} combines role-based trust
management with Datalog constraints, allowing the choice of constraint domain
to control expressiveness. It supplies semantics for query evaluation and
the enforcement engine, together with distributed evaluation. SecPAL's
design~\cite{becker2007secpal} supports constraints, controlled delegation,
recursion, and negated queries. Its authors establish soundness, completeness,
and termination of the evaluation strategy under stated syntactic conditions.
These works thus extend beyond specifying policy syntax: they explicitly
connect logical meaning and evaluation.

Margrave~\cite{fisler2005margrave} analyzes access-control policies using
decision diagrams, including property checking and the effects of policy
changes for a supported subset of XACML. It helps determine which requests
receive which decisions and how those decisions change. The work also
recognizes that policy verification and analysis of the enclosing program
are distinct, related tasks. Policy consistency and the integration of
policy decisions into software are therefore established research concerns,
not questions introduced by this case study.

Our emphasis is a concrete implementation that disagrees with its documented
rule-selection contract and the consequences of abstracting that disagreement
at protocol boundaries. We do not offer a competing authorization language,
a general policy analyzer, or evaluation-correctness results comparable to
SecPAL's. The observed matcher discrepancy has implementation evidence, while
the modeled communication consequence has separate formal evidence. Their
remaining semantic mapping is precisely the connection that must be supplied
before claiming a complete implementation-to-protocol argument.

\subsection{Authentication--Authorization Gaps}

The distinction between identifying a principal and deciding its authority
is longstanding. Lampson, Abadi, Burrows, and Wobber~\cite{lampson1992authentication}
develop authentication theory together with its use in distributed access
control, including principals, secure channels, and delegation. This is
an important counterpoint to treating authentication and authorization as
historically unrelated topics. SPKI~\cite{ellison1999spki} likewise gives
authorization an explicit role in certificate reasoning, while
SecPAL~\cite{becker2007secpal} gives permission derivation a formal execution
strategy. The conceptual distinction is not our novelty claim.

In an agent communication architecture, an authorization decision can become
an input to material release, credential issuance, and eventual message
acceptance. Our question is whether those protocol actions preserve the
intended policy meaning when the decision is computed by software. This
question combines concerns addressed by credential systems, policy analysis,
and protocol verification, but requires evidence at their interfaces. A
correspondence to prior token issuance answers a different question from
whether the token should have been issued under the intended policy.

The SAGA contribution is a bounded case study connecting a confirmed local
authorization inconsistency with formal analysis of its possible protocol
consequence. Here, connecting means relating two explicitly separated evidence
layers, not claiming an established executable refinement. The gate controls
further locate where the modeled communication path can be blocked. We do
not claim priority over all studies of authentication and authorization,
generalize the inconsistency to other agent systems, or report a production
deployment exploit. Strict matcher-to-model mapping remains future work.
