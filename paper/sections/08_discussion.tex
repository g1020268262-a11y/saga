\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Authentication Proofs Are Insufficient}

Authentication, confidentiality, and secure channels provide complementary
foundations for agent communication. Authentication addresses who is
communicating; confidentiality concerns who can learn the protected
information. Neither establishes whether the receiving user's policy
permits that communication. Correctly identifying a participant and
protecting a credential in transit do not establish the participant's
entitlement to receive or use that credential.

The SAGA case study makes this distinction concrete without identifying an
authentication failure. The Stage~1 results retain the three queried
authentication correspondences and token secrecy, while the authorization
diagnostic exposes an obligation absent from that model. Separately, the
bridge-derived consequence model permits message acceptance after an
externally established policy-decision inconsistency is transferred into
its symbolic scenario. This does not indicate cryptographic failure or
invalidate an authentication proof. These results have
different scopes; the Stage~1 guarantees do not automatically transfer to
all later models or to deployments.

As formulated in Chapter~3, accepted contact needs an applicable policy
decision linked to the same participants and credential. Prior issuance
establishes credential provenance, but not permission to issue.
Authentication establishes the identities to which authorization decisions
must be bound; authorization determines what those identities may do.

\subsection{Formal Verification Coverage}

ProVerif verifies modeled properties under explicit assumptions.
Authentication correspondences constrain event origins; secrecy queries
constrain adversarial knowledge. An absent or abstract authorization decision
layer leaves implementation authorization correctness unestablished, without
invalidating the properties actually verified.

The distinction is particularly important in the diagnostic model, where
\texttt{PolicyAllow} is never emitted. Its failed correspondence identifies
missing authorization support in the model; it does not independently detect
the Python defect. Coverage also depends on how security-critical
implementation decisions are connected to their formal abstractions.
In this case study, that connection has three distinct evidentiary roles:
historical function-level observation, checked decision transfer, and
symbolic consequence analysis. The bridge independently evaluates finite
specification semantics, binds an archived implementation observation to
the same policy evaluation, and reconstructs the scenario from the checked
pair. The resulting full model is then executed by ProVerif.

The bridge narrows the evidence gap for the recorded evaluation by removing
hand-entered decision labels. The specification-side Deny comes from the
independent evaluator, while the implementation-side Allow comes from the
archived matcher execution. ProVerif analyzes consequences of the resulting
scenario facts; it neither executes Python nor discovers the implementation
inconsistency. Mechanizing this transfer does not eliminate the abstraction
between concrete deployment roles and symbolic participants or prove
implementation refinement. Historical package D used a preset divergence;
package B reconstructs decision values while preserving the symbolic
consequence structure. B does not retroactively change D's provenance.

Three questions therefore require separate justification: was the decision
computed according to the intended policy; was it correctly consumed at the
relevant enforcement boundary; and do authentication and credential
properties hold once execution proceeds? The third cannot substitute for
the first, while the gate controls show why the second depends on where
enforcement occurs. This distinction organizes the case study's security
argument rather than establishing a universal security theorem.
Correspondences also require
reachability context: they hold vacuously in the denial controls. The concern
is alignment between implementation evidence, model, query, and claim,
not a limitation attributed to the verification tool. The case study
demonstrates a reproducible evidence chain with distinguishable observation,
specification evaluation, checked scenario construction, and symbolic
analysis; it does not introduce a new policy logic or verified compiler.

\subsection{Policy Semantics as Security-Critical State}

Authorization decisions are security-critical derived state: they determine whether
an enforcement point may release material, issue a credential, or accept
communication. Their meaning depends on the relationship between policy
semantics, evaluation, and enforcement. Protecting a decision in transit or
checking the resulting credential does not establish that the evaluator
computed the intended permission.

In this case study, rule ordering, specificity, and budget interpretation
connect the policy to that decision. Specificity defines which matching
rule should determine the result; the observed order dependence shows why
list traversal must preserve that rule-selection contract. The selected
budget then needs an explicit permission interpretation. In the recorded
case, $-1$ denotes denial and $10$ denotes permission. This use of a budget
as a contact decision is distinct from enforcing quota consumption over
subsequent messages, which the evaluated models do not establish.

Explicitly binding the concrete input, specification evaluation,
implementation observation, and formal scenario makes the origin of an
authorization state inspectable. Otherwise, a consequence analysis could
verify properties of a supplied permission without establishing that the
permission follows the intended policy. The bridge addresses decision-value
transfer for the recorded case; correctness of the evaluator and of the
downstream consumers remains a separate obligation.

The gate controls show why the decision's consumer also matters. Receiver
denial blocks issuance and acceptance while Provider release remains
possible; Provider denial blocks the earlier release boundary. These are
results of the gate-control models, not validation of equivalent gates in
a running deployment. Decision correctness and enforcement placement are
distinct questions: which permission was derived, and at which boundary
was it consumed? Policy meaning and the protected action must be connected
at each relevant stage.
These observations motivate treating policy evaluation as security-critical
code. They neither establish a deployed repair nor imply that all policy
engines contain the observed inconsistency.

\subsection{Generalization Beyond SAGA}

SAGA is a case study, not a representative sample of all agentic systems.
Programmable authorization components create consistency requirements
between the policy they express, the decisions they compute, and the actions
that consume those decisions. The SAGA observation motivates a verification
question: when executable policy code derives a decision that another
component later consumes, how is the intended policy meaning preserved
across that boundary?
Agent frameworks, API gateways, capability systems, and policy engines offer
contexts in which this separation may warrant examination. This relevance
concerns the verification question, not evidence of a shared authorization
inconsistency.

Applying this perspective requires evidence connecting local policy semantics,
evaluation, and enforcement under each system's assumptions. We have not
verified these systems for the same inconsistency or consequence; prevalence
and impact beyond SAGA remain unmeasured. Likewise, the bridge is a
case-specific evidence-transfer mechanism, not a general verification
framework validated for arbitrary systems.

\subsection{Limitations}

\paragraph{Implementation-to-model correspondence.}
For the recorded evaluation, the bridge mechanizes decision-value transfer
from concrete policy input and an archived implementation observation to
the executed symbolic scenario. Independent specification evaluation,
provenance binding, checked-pair construction, and scenario reconstruction
are completed parts of that chain. However, evaluator correctness is not
theorem-proved, and its finite pattern grammar does not cover complete
Python or \texttt{fnmatch} semantics. This is neither full formal verification
of the matcher implementation nor correctness for arbitrary policies.

The model represents the concrete initiator by \texttt{aid\_B} and the
unobserved receiver by \texttt{aid\_A}, with \texttt{BuggyPolicy} as a local
scenario label. These bindings remain modeling abstractions. Correspondence
with deployed Provider and receiver consumers, including equivalence of
their behavior to the model, is not established. General refinement between
the Python implementation and the symbolic protocol remains unproved.

\paragraph{Deployment evidence.}
Direct matcher calls provide observations at the function level, not
executions of a deployed service. No deployed-service exploit, remote
attack, or malicious infrastructure experiment is demonstrated. The archive
does not identify deployed receiver or policy ownership, or establish that
the matcher observation and symbolic witness describe the same concrete
service session. The symbolic witness follows prescribed peer
processes, without establishing arbitrary attacker control or completion of
downstream tool actions. These limits qualify communication impact, not the
confirmed matcher discrepancy.

\paragraph{Model abstraction.}
The private \texttt{tls\_chat} channel abstracts authenticated, confidential
transport rather than verifying TLS. The handoff represented by the
\texttt{ProviderReleased} table is also abstract.
Although issuance and acceptance are modeled, the complete token lifecycle
is not: expiry, revocation, policy updates, and quota consumption are outside
the evaluated properties. Concurrency is represented by replicated processes
and shared token state, not by the concrete service scheduling and storage
implementation. The witness can involve different issuing and accepting
process instances; fresh tokens do not establish session isolation or
injective agreement. The models therefore do not simulate a complete
deployment or establish the correctness of these omitted behaviors.
Neither D nor B includes a standalone \texttt{TokenIssue} reachability
query: issuance in a witness and the acceptance-to-issuance correspondence
must not be reported as such an additional query result.

\paragraph{Single case study.}
The evidence concerns SAGA and the evaluated scenarios, not deployment
prevalence or current upstream vulnerability status. Bridge v1.1.1
demonstrates reproducible evidence transfer for one recorded policy
evaluation, not validation over a large policy corpus. Normal allow/deny
controls and the reversed-order check support the bridge evaluation;
they are not a deployment-scale sample. Assessing broader applicability
requires additional agent-system studies, each with its own implementation
evidence and modeling assumptions.
