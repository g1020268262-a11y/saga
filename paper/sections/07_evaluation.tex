\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Evaluation Questions}

We evaluate evidence-backed authorization decision analysis for the recorded
SAGA case through four questions using archived evidence:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[Q1.] Does the recorded matcher decision agree with the documented
most-specific policy semantics?
\item[Q2.] Do the original authentication and secrecy queries establish
the authorization relation required by this case study?
\item[Q3.] Which symbolic consequences are reachable in the divergence
scenario? Separately, which stages are blocked by the preset Provider and
receiver permissions in the independent gate-control models?
\item[Q4.] Can the analysis procedure check and transfer the specification-side
decision and implementation-observed decision into the fixed Deny/Allow
scenario, tracking provenance without per-run manual transcription?
\end{enumerate}
Q4 concerns evidence transfer and artifact integrity, not a new security
theorem. Implementation observations, bridge checks, and symbolic results
retain distinct evidentiary roles. This evaluation reports already archived
executions; no new experiment is performed for this paper revision.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

The matcher record uses Python~3.14.3 for direct function calls.
The formal records use ProVerif~2.05 on Windows. Four evidence packages
provide the inputs and results: Stage~1 (S), a historical baseline scenario
with preset divergence (D), a bridge-derived decision divergence scenario (B),
and independent gate-control analysis (G).

Their manifests record execution metadata, source revisions, commands,

\subsection{Matcher Evaluation}

The intended semantics select the most specific matching rule. The
self-contained \texttt{proofs/test\_matcher\_bug.py} illustrates the
inconsistency with a specific deny rule for
\texttt{mallory@example.com:*} followed by a wildcard allow rule.
This explanatory script defines its own matcher variants and simplified
specificity function; it does not import the implementation matcher.

Package S supplies direct implementation evidence in
\texttt{policy-\allowbreak matcher-\allowbreak sanity.json}.
Its recorded invocation uses
\texttt{alice@example.com:agent}, a specific rule
\texttt{alice@example.com:*} with budget $-1$, and a wildcard rule with
budget $10$. The rulebook is valid, and the recorded specificity scores
are $70$ and $0$. The documented semantics therefore require budget $-1$
in either ordering. Table~\ref{tab:matcher-evaluation} reports the saved
results for these two orderings.

The first ordering establishes a concrete policy decision mismatch; the
second shows that reordering the same rules changes the result. This is
an implementation-level, order-dependent authorization decision inconsistency. The record
covers matcher calls, not Provider processing, token issuance, or message
acceptance in a running service. Two observed orderings do not estimate
the prevalence of the defect across rulebooks or deployments.

For the same ordered rulebook, B's independent finite evaluator selects
\texttt{r0}, the unique highest-specificity match, with score $70$ and
budget $-1$. Its specification decision is therefore Deny; the bound
implementation observation supplies budget $10$, classified as Allow.
The archive's historical expected-result field is not used as the evaluator's
answer. This independently derived decision and the direct observation
provide the two sides of the recorded inconsistency.

\subsection{Evidence-Backed Decision Transformation}

Package B addresses Q4 by evaluating the decision-bridge component of the
analysis procedure for the recorded case. It checks the independently derived
Deny decision and observed Allow decision against the recorded ordered rules, matched identity, and
historical source provenance. Rechecking that pair reconstructs the
\texttt{ScenarioSpecDeny} and \texttt{ScenarioImplAllow} table facts in the
full model. They remain fixed symbolic inputs during ProVerif execution,
not matcher execution events. Within the fixed Deny/Allow adapter, the
recorded values are transferred without per-run manual transcription.
Policy abstraction, symbolic role mapping, protocol construction, and query
design remain analysis choices as specified in Section~\ref{sec:implementation-mapping}.

The archived ProVerif~2.05 run of this derived model exits with status zero
and reports exactly the two expected queries: \texttt{ChatAccept} is
reachable, and acceptance implies prior \texttt{TokenIssue}. The manifest
records bridge v1.1.1, a clean pre-run code revision, and unchanged tracked
files after execution. Its hashes bind the archived observation, decision
pair, template, generated scenario and full model, and verifier executable
and outputs. The separate run-start record, generated input, command,
exit status, and final manifest document this execution. Hashes provide
integrity binding, not independent attestation of archive authenticity.

Separately, the saved test log reports 25 tests passed with exit status zero.
These cover historical replay independent of current source equality;
rejection of missing historical revisions and wrong blobs; explicit
line-ending provenance; decision-pair tampering; template-hash mismatch;
missing or duplicate scenario regions; protocol, query, channel, and token
mutations; and enforcement of the approved comment changes. They also
cover normal allow/deny and reversed-order controls, together with
unsupported patterns, ties, and invalid inputs. These are artifact and
reproducibility checks, not 25 security properties or a proof of the evaluator.
Their success supports the checked transfer for this recorded case, not
general implementation-to-model refinement.

\subsection{Formal Verification Results}

\paragraph{Authorization coverage gap.}
The original communication model in S produces eleven results: seven
event-reachability results, three authentication correspondences, and one
token-secrecy result. The diagnostic model preserves these results and
adds the authorization correspondence
\[
\mathsf{Accept}(R,I,t)\Longrightarrow\mathsf{PolicyAllow}(R,I).
\]
This added correspondence is false, while the three authentication
correspondences and token secrecy remain true. Here, \texttt{Accept}
denotes receipt and verification of a token by the initiating peer;
it is not application-message acceptance.

The diagnostic process never emits \texttt{PolicyAllow}; its
\texttt{PolicyDeny} event records denial without guarding execution. Consequently,
the failed correspondence exposes an authorization obligation absent from
the original verification; it does not independently demonstrate a
matcher execution error. The retained authentication and secrecy results
apply to these Stage~1 queries. They are not automatically inherited by
the later communication models.

\paragraph{Historical baseline scenario (D).}
Package D contains two final results for the preset \texttt{turepass} scenario.
The query asserting that \texttt{ChatAccept} is unreachable is false,
with a reconstructed formal reachability witness. Under preset
\texttt{ScenarioSpecDeny} and \texttt{ScenarioImplAllow} facts, the
witness contains intended denial, material release, token issuance, and
application-message acceptance. This is a symbolic reachability
consequence of the divergence abstraction.

The second result establishes
\[
\mathsf{ChatAccept}(R,I,t,m)\Rightarrow\mathsf{TokenIssue}(R,I,t).
\]
Thus, acceptance can coexist with the assumed policy denial while still
having corresponding prior token issuance. Credential provenance and
policy permission remain separate obligations. Issuance is observed in
the witness; this model has no standalone \texttt{TokenIssue} reachability
query and no \texttt{ChatAccept}--\texttt{ChatSend} correspondence query.
The complete D archive supplies these two final results with independent
execution provenance, beyond the historical \texttt{turetrace} excerpt.

\paragraph{Bridge-derived decision divergence scenario (B).}
The separate execution archived in B obtains the same two outcomes after
reconstructing the scenario from the checked pair. Protocol statements
and queries are intentionally preserved, so matching outcomes are expected;
output agreement alone does not prove bridge correctness or execution
freshness. B adds no query. As in D, token issuance occurs in the witness,
but there is no standalone \texttt{TokenIssue} reachability query or
acceptance-to-send query. The new evidence connects recorded decision values
to the scenario used in this execution without turning the symbolic witness
into a running-service observation.

\paragraph{Independent gate-control analysis (G).}
Package G evaluates three fixed pairs of Provider and receiver permissions.
These inputs are preset enforcement conditions, not new observations of
matcher decision divergence. The comparison isolates modeled blocking
positions within G; it does not test a fix to the inconsistency recorded in S.
Each control has three reachability queries and two correspondences.
Table~\ref{tab:gate-evaluation} translates the reachability results into
reachable (R) or unreachable (U), avoiding ambiguity about negated-query
truth values.

Within these gate-control models, Provider denial prevents release and
downstream actions. Receiver denial prevents issuance and acceptance, while
Provider release remains reachable.
The allow/allow control establishes a reachable permitted path in the symbolic model.
Both acceptance-to-issuance and acceptance-to-send correspondences are
true in all three controls. In the denial cases they hold vacuously,
because acceptance is unreachable; only the allow/allow case supplies
a non-vacuous control. These experiments locate blocking stages within
the model, rather than evaluate a deployed repair. The controls use a
fixed private token, unlike the fresh tokens in D and B, and are not verified
repairs or refinements of either divergence model.

\subsection{Security Findings Summary}

The evidence supports four complementary findings. First, direct matcher
calls demonstrate a decision inconsistent with documented specificity
semantics. Second, the coverage diagnostic distinguishes authorization
from the authentication and secrecy properties actually queried in
Stage~1. Third, independent finite specification evaluation and a
provenance-bound observation yield a checked Deny/Allow pair for the same
recorded policy evaluation; the bridge reconstructs the formal scenario
from that pair. Fourth, the fresh derived-model execution reaches
\texttt{ChatAccept} while establishing its correspondence to prior issuance;
the independent gate controls identify distinct release and issuance boundaries.

Together, these findings support evidence-backed analysis of the recorded
SAGA authorization decision inconsistency. The implementation observation
and specification-side evaluation establish the local discrepancy; the bridge
component supplies checked evidence transfer into B. B characterizes a
conditional symbolic consequence, while G separately characterizes modeled
enforcement positions. Neither the reachability results nor the gate controls
establish complete authorization correctness or a verified fix. This is
neither a running-service reproduction, a refinement proof, nor a
deployment-scale security evaluation. No evidence layer substitutes for
another. No result
establishes cryptographic primitive failure, certificate forgery, key
compromise, or arbitrary attacker control of the modeled peer processes.

\subsection{Limitations}

The procedure provides executable decision transfer with provenance checks
for the recorded policy input, but does not prove refinement between Python
and the symbolic protocol model. The external evaluator covers a finite
pattern grammar; its correctness is not theorem-proved. Neither complete
Python or \texttt{fnmatch} semantics nor correctness for arbitrary policies
is established. ProVerif does not execute the matcher. The evaluated
properties do not discharge Chapter~3's ideal decision-to-access justification
relation or establish authorization correctness in real service executions.

Mapping the concrete evaluation to \texttt{aid\_B}, \texttt{aid\_A}, and
\texttt{BuggyPolicy} remains an analysis abstraction. The historical
function observation identifies neither a deployed receiver nor a deployed
policy owner, and is not evidence of the same concrete service session as
the symbolic witness. Production Provider and receiver consumers, their
equivalence to the modeled consumers, and their use of the recorded
decision have not been validated by this bridge.

The model abstracts both transport and handoff through its private
\texttt{tls\_chat} channel and \texttt{ProviderReleased} table, respectively.
The evaluated queries do not
establish token expiry, quota
consumption, revocation, policy-update behavior, session isolation, or
injective agreement. No complete production-service reproduction or
deployment-scale measurement is reported. The historical observations do
not establish current upstream vulnerability status or prevalence across
deployments. Future work may establish stronger semantic correspondence
and evaluate service behavior and other agent systems; the present
case study does not establish those outcomes or generality.
