\section{Formalization}
\label{sec:formalization}

\subsection{Modeling Approach}

The formal model analyzes conditional consequences of authorization decision
divergence for the recorded SAGA case. An archived implementation observation
and independent specification-side evaluation yield a checked decision pair.
An evidence-backed decision transformation procedure reconstructs a fixed
symbolic scenario from that pair, which ProVerif then analyzes under explicit
protocol assumptions (Figure~\ref{fig:implementation-mapping}). The decision
bridge is a component of this analysis procedure, not a formal model
translation framework. It does not construct the protocol abstraction or
establish refinement between Python and the symbolic model. ProVerif neither
executes Python nor discovers the implementation inconsistency. This analysis
retains Chapter~3's fixed-policy setting and studies selected consequences;
it does not prove the ideal decision-to-access justification relation.

\subsection{Protocol Model}

The Provider abstracts registration checks, participant authentication, and
the authorization-related release of contact material. \texttt{PeerA}, the
receiver $R$, issues tokens and receives application messages.
\texttt{PeerB}, the initiator $I$, requests a token and sends a message after
receiving it. A CA process supplies certificates. Replicated processes use
symbolic cryptography and public communication; the private
\texttt{tls\_chat} channel abstracts authenticated, confidential transport.

We distinguish the divergence model, \texttt{turepass}, from three independent
gate controls. The controls differ only in their preset gate permissions.
They use a fixed private token, whereas \texttt{turepass} generates fresh
tokens. The controls are not verified repairs or a refinement of that model.
The current bridge-derived model preserves the consequence structure of
\texttt{turepass}, with scenario input reconstructed before verification;
the historical and bridge-derived executions have separate evidence packages.

\subsection{Evidence-Backed Decision Transformation Procedure}
\label{sec:implementation-mapping}

\emph{Recorded input and provenance.} Bridge v1.1.1 checks an ordered policy
$P$, its matched initiating identity $I$, a policy hash, the historical
matcher revision and source hash, and the archived observation's location
and hash. These fields track the recorded inputs and evidence used in the
comparison; they do not identify a real authorization event or protocol
session. The matched identity is the initiator, not the receiver.
Policy labels and rule identifiers organize the artifact; they do not
establish deployed policy ownership.

\emph{Independent specification decision.} An executable evaluator supports
the finite pattern subset needed for the recorded case: \texttt{*},
lowercase ASCII literal AIDs, and literal UIDs followed by \texttt{:*}.
It independently computes matching and the numerical specificity described
in Chapter~4, then selects the unique highest-scoring match. It neither
imports production \texttt{match()} nor reads the archive's expected result
as an answer. Budgets are classified as Negative, Zero, or Positive; only
Positive grants contact permission. No match yields Zero. Tied highest
scores and unsupported patterns produce \texttt{Unsupported}; malformed
inputs produce \texttt{Invalid}. Neither status is silently interpreted as
denial. This is finite executable specification semantics, not theorem-proved
evaluator correctness or complete \texttt{fnmatch} semantics.

\emph{Implementation observation.} The archived matcher record contains a direct
function-level call to the unmodified matcher implementation for
\texttt{alice@\allowbreak example.com:\allowbreak agent}. A specific denial followed
by general permission returns $10$; the reversed order returns $-1$.
The bridge loads the observation for the selected order, checks its identity
and ordered rules, and binds its source hash to the historical Git revision
using explicit line-ending encodings. Equality with the current checkout is
not required. The observed implementation decision is an archived execution result,
not a prediction by the evaluator or an execution inside ProVerif.

\emph{Decision-pair construction.} For these checked recorded inputs, let
$D_{\mathrm{spec}}(P,I)$ be the independently computed specification decision
and $D_{\mathrm{impl}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(P,I)$ the archived matcher result
interpreted using Chapter~3's positive-budget convention. The evaluator
selects the specific rule, with score $70$ rather than $0$ and budget $-1$;
the bound observation supplies budget $10$. Thus,
\[
 (D_{\mathrm{spec}}(P,I),D_{\mathrm{impl}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(P,I))
       =(\mathsf{Deny},\mathsf{Allow}).
\]
The procedure compares the decisions only after validating the recorded
ordered rules, matched identity, and observation provenance. This establishes
an evidence-linked comparison, not equivalence of real executions. The
observation-based relation does not define implementation outputs for
arbitrary inputs.

\emph{Scenario reconstruction.} Before generating the full model, the bridge
recomputes the specification decision, reloads and validates the observation,
and rechecks their context; saved decision labels are not trusted. It locates
a unique scenario region in the hash-pinned consequence template and joins
the retained prefix and suffix around the generated statements in
Table~\ref{tab:implementation-mapping}. The executed model reconstructs its
authorization-divergence scenario region from the checked pair, while
protocol processes, events, channels, token logic, and queries remain frozen.
Only the provenance header, scenario region, and three approved comment-only
normalizations may differ. Exact structural checks and mutation tests support
this artifact-level checked transformation, not semantic refinement.

\emph{Automation and analysis choices.} Within the supported finite input
grammar, the procedure automates specification-side decision evaluation,
observation validation, provenance checks, and decision-pair comparison.
Scenario generation is restricted to the checked Deny/Allow pair and the
fixed consequence template; other decision pairs do not generate a full
model through this adapter. Policy abstraction, including the supported
grammar and budget interpretation, remains an analysis choice. Symbolic role
mapping, protocol model construction, and query design are also specified by
the analyst rather than derived from the observation. The independent gate
controls use analyst-selected Provider/receiver permission combinations;
they are not additional implementation-observed decision pairs.

\emph{Remaining abstraction boundary.} The concrete initiating identity is
represented by \texttt{aid\_B}; the unobserved receiving policy owner by
\texttt{aid\_A}; and the policy evaluation by the scenario-local label
\texttt{BuggyPolicy}. These role bindings remain analysis abstractions.
The matcher archive identifies neither a deployed receiver nor a deployed
policy owner, and does not describe the same concrete service session as the
symbolic witness. Both Provider and receiver consume the fixed allow fact;
their handoff and behavior are not validated by the matcher observation.
Avoiding per-run manual transcription within this fixed adapter therefore
does not remove the implementation-to-model refinement boundary.

\subsection{Authorization Events and State}

In both the historical and bridge-derived models, \texttt{ScenarioSpecDeny}
and \texttt{ScenarioImplAllow} are initialized table facts, not Python
matcher events or ProVerif-computed policy decisions. The facts remain fixed
symbolic inputs during ProVerif execution. In the bridge-derived run only,
their decision values are reconstructed externally from the checked pair.
The historical \texttt{turepass} execution used
a manually stated divergence scenario. These separate evidence packages
must not be conflated. The facts share a scenario identifier and initiating identity.
\texttt{SpecIntendedDeny}$(I,R)$ records the specification-side denial represented
in the symbolic scenario. The following
protocol events use receiver-first arguments; $t$ denotes the wire token.

The \texttt{ProviderReleased} table abstracts a handoff to receiver processing.

issuance. Receiver processing also requires the handoff state. These tables
govern execution; recording an event alone does not grant or deny permission.

\subsection{Verification Queries}

\emph{Reachability.} Both divergence models query whether
\texttt{ChatAccept} can occur. The controls separately query
\texttt{ProviderRelease}, \texttt{TokenIssue}, and \texttt{ChatAccept} for
the fixed peer identities. A false \texttt{not event(...)} result with a
reconstructed witness establishes an existential modeled consequence under
the scenario assumptions, not a security guarantee. There is no independent
\texttt{TokenIssue} reachability query in either historical \texttt{turepass}
or its bridge-derived execution.

\emph{Correspondence.} Both divergence models and the three controls query
\[
\mathsf{ChatAccept}(R,I,t,m)\Longrightarrow\mathsf{TokenIssue}(R,I,t).
\]
The controls also query
\[
\mathsf{ChatAccept}(R,I,t,m)\Longrightarrow\mathsf{ChatSend}(R,I,t,m).
\]
Variables are universally quantified. These non-injective correspondences
require prior issuance for the same participants and token, or prior sending
of the same message, respectively. They do not establish policy permission,
unique sessions, or Chapter~3's full decision-to-access justification.

\emph{Gate placement.} The controls preset Provider/receiver permissions to
allow/allow, deny/allow, and allow/deny. Comparing their three reachability
queries locates blocking stages under the shared handoff abstraction.

\subsection{Formal Results}

The historical \texttt{turepass} archive reports message acceptance reachable
under its preset divergence and the acceptance-to-issuance correspondence
true. Separately, a fresh ProVerif~2.05 execution of the bridge v1.1.1 derived
model reports the same two query outcomes after scenario reconstruction:
\texttt{ChatAccept} is reachable, and acceptance implies prior
\texttt{TokenIssue}. The new archive binds the input decision pair, generated
model, clean code revision, and verifier output. It does not retroactively
upgrade the historical execution or constitute a production-service run.
Token issuance occurs in the witness; neither divergence model has a
standalone issuance-reachability query.

For the controls, the results are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\hline
Provider / Receiver & Release & Issue & Accept \\
\hline
Allow / Allow & Reachable & Reachable & Reachable \\
Deny / Allow & Unreachable & Unreachable & Unreachable \\
Allow / Deny & Reachable & Unreachable & Unreachable \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Provider denial stops release and downstream events. Receiver denial stops
issuance and message acceptance after material release remains possible.
Both control correspondences are true in all three scenarios. They are
non-vacuous on the allow/allow path; in each denial scenario, acceptance is
unreachable and the correspondences hold vacuously.

These results are documented in archived summaries and raw output.
The historical divergence package D records its command, version,
input hash, and zero exit status for an independent historical run, separate
from the earlier \texttt{turetrace} excerpt.\footnote{Evidence under

20260919T090826295518Z/} and \texttt{authz-chat-gates-\allowbreak
20260918T023621017067Z/}.}
The bridge-derived execution is recorded separately in
\texttt{authz-bridge-turepass-\allowbreak 20260922T111657792091Z/}

\subsection{Formalization Boundary}

The matcher inconsistency is demonstrated at implementation level, and
ProVerif models its protocol-level consequence under explicit assumptions.
The external evaluator computes finite specification decisions, and the bridge
checks their connection to archived implementation observations and generated
scenario input. These executable checks are not proofs of evaluator correctness,
complete Python or \texttt{fnmatch} semantics, or arbitrary policy correctness.
ProVerif establishes reachability, blocking, and correspondences only within
the stated symbolic scenarios; it does not prove Python execution correctness
or a production deployment result.
In particular, provenance tracking does not prove Python matcher correctness,
complete SAGA authorization correctness, or equivalence between the recorded
function execution and the symbolic witness. Chapter~3's full justification
relation remains an ideal objective rather than a verified property here.

The prescribed peer processes do not represent arbitrary application behavior
under attacker control. Private transport and shared state remain abstractions;
token expiry, quota consumption, revocation, and policy updates are not
covered. No cryptographic primitive failure or key compromise is established.
The recorded correspondences are not a revalidation of every original
authentication or secrecy property. For the fixed Deny/Allow adapter,
per-run decision transfer is automated, but symbolic role binding, correspondence with
deployed consumers, general implementation-to-model refinement, and
service-level reproduction remain outside the completed evidence. The bridge
implements an evidence-backed decision transformation procedure for this case,
not a verified compiler or a new general verification framework.
