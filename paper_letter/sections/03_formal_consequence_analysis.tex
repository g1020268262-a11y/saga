\section{Formal Authorization Analysis}
\label{sec:formalization}

\paragraph{Authorization and verification coverage.}
Formal consequence analysis distinguishes token origin from policy-compliant
issuance. In the consequence model, the verified non-injective correspondence
is
\[
 \mathsf{ChatAccept}(R,I,t,m)
 \Longrightarrow \mathsf{TokenIssue}(R,I,t)
\]
where $R$ is the receiver and token issuer, and $I$ is the initiator. Prior
issuance must match $R$, $I$, and encrypted token $t$. The distinct ideal
authorization condition for a static receiver policy is
\[
 \mathsf{TokenIssue}(R,I,t)
 \Longrightarrow \mathsf{Allow}_{\mathrm{spec}}(P_R,I),
\]
where $P_R$ is $R$'s contact policy and \(\mathsf{Allow}_{\mathrm{spec}}\)
denotes permission under its intended semantics, rather than the matcher
result. This second formula is a desired condition, neither an executed
ProVerif query nor a proved theorem.

The archived ProVerif 2.05 diagnostic preserves SAGA's original authentication
and token-secrecy results but reports an added prior-\texttt{PolicyAllow}
correspondence for \texttt{Accept} as false. Here \texttt{Accept} denotes token
receipt and verification, distinct from \texttt{ChatAccept}.
\texttt{PolicyAllow} is not connected to actual policy enforcement. The
failure exposes a proof-coverage gap; it neither executes the Python matcher
nor independently identifies its defect.

\paragraph{Evidence-linked scenario construction.}
\label{sec:implementation-mapping}
The Decision Bridge independently evaluates the recorded policy and checks
the archived production matcher observation against the ordered rules and
initiator identity. It verifies observation hashes and historical source
binding before forming the checked Deny/Allow decision pair. That pair
reconstructs only the designated scenario region of a hash-pinned ProVerif
template, preserving its protocol semantics and queries. This makes the
transfer of recorded decision evidence checkable and reproducible.

The Bridge supports only the finite policy grammar needed for this case. It
proves neither evaluator correctness for arbitrary policies nor
Python-to-ProVerif refinement, and is not a general program translator.
ProVerif takes the checked divergence as scenario input; it does not execute
Python or the production matcher. Only authorization decisions and
token-lifecycle events link the implementation and symbolic evidence.

\paragraph{Symbolic consequences.}
The initiator has a valid registered identity, follows the prescribed symbolic
peer process, and cannot alter the receiver's fixed policy. The Provider and
Certificate Authority (CA) are trusted, cryptographic checks are effective,
and private keys are uncompromised. A private symbolic channel abstracts
authenticated, confidential transport; reachability is not an
arbitrary-adversary claim.

\texttt{TokenIssue}$(R,I,t)$ follows \texttt{ActiveToken} storage of $R$, $I$,
encrypted token $t$, and $I$'s public access-control key, and precedes
transmission. For private-channel message $m$, \texttt{ChatAccept}$(R,I,t,m)$
occurs only after checking the fixed identity and an \texttt{ActiveToken}
entry matching $R$, $I$, $t$, and $I$'s sender public key.

Under the fixed Deny/Allow divergence, the archived run reports
\texttt{ChatAccept} reachable and, separately, the token-origin correspondence
true. Its witness includes issuance; no standalone \texttt{TokenIssue}
reachability query exists. Token-origin consistency thus coexists with
intended-policy denial. Symbolic message acceptance and controlled consumer
execution remain separate evidence layers, not a complete service-level
end-to-end attack.
