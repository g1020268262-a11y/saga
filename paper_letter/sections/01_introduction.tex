\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

LLM-based agents increasingly use external tools and
services~\cite{wang2024agents,debenedetti2024agentdojo}.
Authentication establishes identity; receiver-side authorization determines
whether the identified agent may communicate~\cite{ellison1999spki}.
Authentication and token secrecy leave policy-compliant authorization as a
separate security requirement.

SAGA~\cite{syros2025saga} uses contact policies and access-control tokens to
govern communication between registered agents. Its production matcher
implements the receiver's policy decision. We investigate authorization
decision consistency and its security consequences by asking:
\emph{Can an implementation-level mismatch with the receiver's intended
contact policy authorize a denied agent, and how far can the resulting
permission propagate through the communication protocol?}

Our analysis combines independent specification decision comparison,
ProVerif analysis of an evidence-linked fixed divergence, and controlled
consumer-path validation. Production matcher observations reveal an
order-dependent inconsistency with the intended most-specific matching
semantics. An authorization coverage diagnostic explains why SAGA's existing
authentication and token-secrecy results leave this requirement unestablished;
it does not identify the implementation defect.

The recorded policy denies while the matcher permits. This permission reaches
token issuance and storage through controlled Provider and receiver paths.
Under the corresponding fixed symbolic divergence, \texttt{ChatAccept} is
reachable while its prior-\texttt{TokenIssue} correspondence holds. These
results distinguish policy-compliant authorization from authenticated
identity and consistent token origin; implementation and symbolic endpoints
remain separate evidence layers.

Our contributions are (1) identification of an authorization decision
inconsistency, explaining the matcher's deviation from most-specific semantics
and confirming the recorded Deny/Allow counterexample with production
observations; (2) formal consequence analysis of an evidence-linked fixed
authorization divergence, using ProVerif to show conditional
\texttt{ChatAccept} reachability alongside a true prior-\texttt{TokenIssue}
correspondence, distinguishing token origin from policy-compliant
authorization; and (3) controlled consumer-path validation of
propagation to token issuance and storage through production Provider and
receiver code with explicit fixtures and controls.

\paragraph{Related work.}
\label{sec:related-work}
Prior work studies authorization languages and policy
semantics~\cite{becker2004cassandra,becker2007secpal} and
policy verification and change-impact analysis~\cite{fisler2005margrave}.
SPKI~\cite{ellison1999spki} and Macaroons~\cite{birgisson2014macaroons}
provide credential- and capability-based authorization abstractions.
ProVerif and Tamarin support symbolic protocol
verification~\cite{blanchet2016proverif,meier2013tamarin}.
Our case analysis connects an observed implementation deviation from policy
semantics to its consumer and symbolic protocol consequences.
