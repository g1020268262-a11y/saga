\section{Authorization Decision Inconsistency}
\label{sec:authorization-inconsistency}

\paragraph{Workflow and policy semantics.}
An initiator requests contact through SAGA's Provider. The receiver's ordered
policy maps identity patterns to integer budgets. Provider and receiver
independently call the production matcher on the initiator. The Provider gates
contact material; the receiver rechecks its policy before token issuance.

For fixed policy $P_R$ and valid identity $I$, the documented contract selects
the matching rule with highest \texttt{aid\_specificity()}. For its unique
budget $b^*$, this study uses
\[
  \mathsf{Allow}_{\mathrm{spec}}(P_R,I)
  \Longleftrightarrow b^*>0.
\]
Positive budgets allow and negative budgets deny. Equal-score conflicts,
changing policies, and zero budgets are outside the cases.

\paragraph{Root cause and observation.}
In the matcher, \texttt{best\_pattern} starts as \texttt{None} but is not
updated when a matching rule changes the budget. Later rules remain compared
with the initial sentinel instead of the best prior match, so a later match can
replace a more specific result. Direct production-matcher calls use a specific
pattern with budget $-1$ and specificity $70$, and wildcard \texttt{*} with
budget $10$ and specificity $0$.

An independent finite specification evaluator selects the specific denial
for both orders. Writing
\[
  \mathsf{Allow}_{\mathrm{impl}}(P_R,I)
  \Longleftrightarrow \mathtt{match}(P_R,I)>0,
\]
the specific-deny/wildcard-allow order yields
$\mathsf{Allow}_{\mathrm{impl}}\neq\mathsf{Allow}_{\mathrm{spec}}$.
The reverse order returns the specified denial, establishing order dependence.
Table~\ref{tab:matcher-evaluation} describes rule order; the D/A label in
Section~\ref{sec:consumer-propagation} instead denotes specification and
implementation decisions. These calls establish only a local decision
inconsistency, not deployment exploitation, message acceptance, or whole-system
failure.
