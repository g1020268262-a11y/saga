\section{Authorization Decision Inconsistency}
\label{sec:authorization-inconsistency}

\paragraph{Intended authorization semantics.}
SAGA's Provider handles contact requests under the receiver's ordered policy,
which maps identity patterns to integer budgets. The Provider and receiver
independently call the production matcher: the Provider gates one-time key
(OTK) release, and the receiver rechecks authorization before token issuance.

SAGA, Section~IV-D.1~\cite{syros2025saga}, specifies selection of the
most-specific matching rule; \texttt{aid\_specificity()} scores the patterns.
A positive budget permits the request; a negative budget denies it.
We consider fixed policies and valid identities, excluding equal-specificity
conflicts and zero budgets.

\paragraph{Root cause and observation.}
The tested production matcher is \texttt{match()} in
\texttt{saga/common/\allowbreak contact\_policy.py}. The artifact binds the
archived matcher observation to pinned upstream source provenance.\footnote{\raggedright Reproduction artifact:
\texttt{github.com/g1020268262-a11y/}\linebreak
\texttt{SAGA-Authorization-Analysis-Artifact}.}
Its \texttt{best\_pattern} starts at \texttt{None}, whose specificity is $-1$.
A more-specific matching rule updates the budget but never
\texttt{best\_pattern}. Later matches still compare against $-1$, allowing
a less-specific wildcard to overwrite the budget.
In the recorded case, \texttt{aid\_specificity()} scores
\texttt{alice@example.com:*} as $70$
and \texttt{*} as $0$; their budgets are $-1$ and $10$, respectively.

The independent finite specification evaluator selects denial for both
orders in Table~\ref{tab:matcher-evaluation}. Specific deny followed by
wildcard allow changes the specified $-1$ into an observed $10$; reversing
the order restores denial. This counterexample establishes an
implementation-level authorization decision inconsistency for the recorded
policy, with permission dependent on rule order.
