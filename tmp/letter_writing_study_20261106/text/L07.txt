SUMMARY This paper ﬁrst takes IoT as an example to provide the
motivation for eliminating the single point of trust (SPOT) in a CA-based
private PKI. It then describes a distributed public key certiﬁcate-issuing
infrastructure that eliminates the SPOT and its limitation derived from gen-
erating signing keys. Finally, it proposes a method to address its limitation
by all certiﬁcate issuers.
key words: authentication, public key infrastructure, distributed ledger
technology
1. Introduction
The Internet of Things (IoT) is not just a mechanism for
connecting devices to the Internet but for realizing advanced
applications through the interconnection of many of these
heterogeneous devices. For example, the IoT will trans-
form homes into smart homes, power grids into smart grids,
and cities into smart cities. IoT systems have the poten-
tial to transform social infrastructures, and it is reasonable
to assume that they will be widely implemented in society
through building consortium of various stakeholders. While
the widespread of devices in every corner of society will
enable seamless service provision, it will also expand the
possible areas of attack. Hence, it is essential to ensure the
security of each IoT device.
Authentication is one of the most important aspects
to consider for enhancing the security of inter-device com-
munication [1]. Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) is widely
known as an authentication mechanism for the Internet. In a
certiﬁcate authority (CA) based PKI, a CA, which is a single
point of trust (SPOT), issues public key certiﬁcates to bind
public keys and subjects expressed by any identities such as
domain names, URIs, or e-mail addresses. There are two
types of CAs: a public CA and a private CA. A public CA
is operated by a publicly trusted party, and it is suitable for
publicly known subjects such as web servers. For example,
a public CA validates a domain name of a web server and is-
sues a certiﬁcate binding a public key to that domain. On the
other hand, a private CA is operated by a private party and
is suitable for known subjects within a private service oper-

more suitable for client authentication, while a public CA is
more suitable for server authentication.
Considering the features of CA-based PKI, private
CAs would be more suitable for authenticating IoT devices.
However, in light of the expansion of consortium-based IoT
systems involving multiple stakeholders, the challenge is
how to operate a private CA without relying on a speciﬁc
party in the consortium. We have proposed a distributed
public key certiﬁcate-issuing infrastructure (DCII) [2] that
uses distributed ledger technology (DLT) to address the
problem of a SPOT for CA-based PKI in private environ-
ments where devices are interconnected. In the DCII, CA
functions are shared among parties as smart contracts, and
logs of the process are recorded in a distributed ledger (DL)
to improve CA transparency. However, there were limita-
tions in terms of transparency and availability due to the
need for a administrator at generating signing keys and the
need to execute smart contracts at verifying certiﬁcates.
In this paper, we provide an overview of the DCII and
propose a method of enclosing signing keys through the co-
operation of all certiﬁcate issuers without depending on a
administrator. The transparency and availability of the DCII
are improved by eliminating the need for the administrator
to generate signing keys.
2. Preliminaries and Problem Statement
2.1 Distributed Public Key Certiﬁcate-Issuing Infrastruc-
ture
When constructing a CA-based PKI with multiple parties,
there are two possible approaches: constructing a single CA
trusted by all parties and coordinating CAs managed indi-
vidually by each party. In the former approach, a single CA
deploys certiﬁcates to all devices. Each party owning de-
vices trusts a single party operating the CA. Then, the oper-
ating party deploys certiﬁcates to devices on behalf of all the
parties. Since this approach trusts a single operating party,
unauthorized devices can participate in the network if unau-
thorized certiﬁcates are issued due to fraud or compromise
of the operating party. In the latter approach, each party
owning devices operates a CA and deploys certiﬁcates to its
own devices. By cooperating with each other, the devices

There is no need to place a point of trust on a speciﬁc party
since this approach is based on the cooperation of multiple
parties. However, since all parties can be attack surfaces,
compromising one of them will allow unauthorized devices
to participate in the PKI. Although each party should have
the same level of security measures in place, the problem of
the weakest link [3] arises.
We have proposed the DCII that employs Hyperledger
Fabric, which is an open-source permissioned DLT, to ad-
dress the problem of a SPOT for a CA-based PKI in private
environments where devices are interconnected. This infras-
tructure has the following two features to realize certiﬁcate
management that does not depend on the operating party of
the CA.
 Certiﬁcate management (issuance, validation, and re-
vocation) is implemented as smart contracts and shared
among all parties.
 Signed keys are enclosed in a DL protected by Intel
SGX and cannot be retrieved.
By enclosing signing keys in a manner that they cannot be
retrieved from the DL, they cannot be used for arbitrary pur-
poses, and the use of signing keys can be restricted to pre-
deﬁned processes.
Figure 1 shows an overview of the DCII. The infras-
tructure manager (IM) ﬁrst sets up the DLT system and de-
ploys smart contracts. The IM then generates two types of
signing keys: one for signing certiﬁcates and the other for
signing match results which prove that certiﬁcates is stored
in the DL. The IM ﬁnally encloses them in the DL. After
the setup is completed, a certiﬁcate issuer (CI), which is a
node in the DLT network, issues a certiﬁcate to a certiﬁcate
holder (CH) using the smart contract and the enclosed sign-
ing key in response to a certiﬁcate signing request from the
CH. When a CH sends a certiﬁcate veriﬁer (CV) the certiﬁ-
cate, the CV request a CI to match the certiﬁcate with the
DL. The CI creates a signed match result using the smart
contract and the enclosed signing key. After conﬁrming the
match result, the CV veriﬁes the signature on the certiﬁcate
and returns the veriﬁcation result to the CH. It is noted that
the IM periodically updates the signing keys.
2.2 Problem Statement and Design Rules
The DCII implements countermeasures against the risk of
unauthorized certiﬁcate issuance by the IM and tampering
with the match result by CIs. First, for the former risk, since
the IM has the signing keys before it is enclosed, there is a
risk of abuse of them. However, certiﬁcates signed without
using the smart contract are not stored in the DL, and this
risk can be mitigated by matching a certiﬁcate with the DL.
Next, for the latter risk, since CIs stand between the smart
contract for matching certiﬁcates and CVs, there is a risk
that a CI falsiﬁes match results. For example, a CI can fal-
sify a legitimate device as an illegal device and vise versa.
Therefore, the DCII guarantees that match results originates

from the DCII by signing the match results with the smart
contract and prevents tampering by CIs. However, the tech-
nique of signing match results of each certiﬁcate match pro-
cess causes the cost of the match process to increase propor-
tionately to the number of devices.
To summarize the above, the match process is im-
plemented to counter fraud by the infrastructure manager.
However, assuming numerous devices, such as in advanced
IoT systems, the match process from all devices becomes a
bottleneck. If the match process is bypassed and only the
certiﬁcate signature is veriﬁed, it is not possible to detect
fraud by the IM. In this paper, we propose a method to ad-
dress this problem. The above problem is caused by the
fact that the IM has the signing key, and it is required that
the signing key is not exposed outside the DL. Therefore,
we design the proposed method with the following design
rules.
 Rule 1: To enclose signing keys into the DL without
exposing them outside the DL in cooperation with all
CIs.
 Rule 2: To update signing keys without exposing them
outside the DL without the cooperation of the parties
involved in the enclosure
Rule 1 is to securely enclose signing keys in a DL without
dependence on a speciﬁc party. Rule 2 is to securely update
signing keys without interference by some parties.
3. Proposed Method
The proposed method prevents signing keys from being ex-
posed outside the DL by generating them within smart con-
tracts. However, smart contracts do not generate signing
keys randomly because they can only operate deterministi-
cally. Hence, the proposed method takes the approach that
the smart contract receives random numbers from all CIs
and generates a signing key based on those numbers. A CI
can only know the signing key if it knows all those random
numbers.

Due to the restriction on using multiple random num-
bers for signing key generation, the proposed method em-
ploys elliptic curve cryptography (ECC) based digital sig-
nature algorithm in which any integer value can be used as
a private key. Especially, this paper adopts the elliptic curve
digital signature algorithm (ECDSA). Compared with RSA,
ECC has more ﬂexibility in choosing a signing key, where
n is a prime number and an integer randomly selected from
[1; n  1] is used as a private key. Though RSA-based digital
signature algorithms can be considered if two prime num-
bers can be computed from the random numbers of the CIs,
they are out of the scope of this paper.

3.4 Issuing Certiﬁcates and Matching Certiﬁcates
In the issuing certiﬁcates and matching certiﬁcates con-
tracts, public key certiﬁcates and matching results are cre-
ated based on the input data. Then, they are signed with
skcert and skmatch. ECDSA uses a random number (nonce)
for signing, so the signature value is non-deterministic. As
explained before, since smart contracts can only implement
deterministic algorithms, the proposed method determinis-
tically calculates a nonce by deterministic ECDSA as spec-
iﬁed in RFC 6979. Digital signatures of public key certiﬁ-
cates and matching results are calculated deterministically
by this scheme. Produced signatures remain fully compati-
ble with plain ECDSA, and the signature veriﬁcation algo-
rithm does not need to be changed.
4. Security Analysis
The adversary aims to pretend that the public key certiﬁcate
of a dishonest IoT device is issued by the DCII, and its goal
is to obtain skmatch and skcert. In this paper, we analyze the
security of the DCII when the IM is an adversary since the
IM has higher privileges to set up the DCII. Since skmatch
and skcert are signing keys generated in the same way, they
will be discussed collectively as sk.
We ﬁrst assume that the DLT system underlying the
proposed method is secure. The underlying cryptographic
primitives are secure, and each DLT node is honest with re-
spect to the underlying consensus model. Furthermore, it
is assumed that Intel SGX functionality is not compromised
and works properly. The IM can collude with CIs, but due
to the distributed nature of DLT, we do not suppose that all
CIs collude with the IM. That is, at least one CI is indepen-
dent of the IM. In addition, we assume some CIs that do not
collude with the IM, thus vulnerable smart contracts cannot
be replaced without those CI being aware of it.

trieve enclosed sk. Therefore, we analyze a guessing attack

5. Conclusion
In this paper, we proposed a method for enclosing signing
keys in the DCII through the cooperation of all CIs. Lever-
aging the property that ECC private keys are integers in a
speciﬁc range, the proposed method encloses and updates
signing keys without exposing them outside the DL. In this
way, the extra certiﬁcate veriﬁcation processes to counter
fraud by the IM can be eliminated. As a result, the applica-
bility of the DCII to systems with numerous devices, such
as advanced IoT systems, would be improved. As a security
evaluation, we analyzed collusion attacks on the part of par-
ties participating in the DCII and proved that the proposed
method is secure against such attacks. The processing time
and ledger size increase for the updating signing key con-
tract are proportional to the number of CIs. In future works,
we will experimentally evaluate the relationship between the
number of CIs and the cost of the updating signing key con-
tract and improve the method not proportional to the number
of CIs.
